8.1 A coefficient is conditional on a specified model

A used car's mileage and age both help predict its price. A simple mileage slope averages over the observed ages at different mileages. A multiple-regression mileage slope instead describes a comparison at the same modeled age and other included predictors. These need not be the same comparison, so different coefficient values do not by themselves indicate software disagreement.

Let $y\in\mathbb R^n$ collect observed responses. Let $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times q}$ be the design matrix, with row $x_i^{\mathsf T}$ describing observation $i$. An intercept is represented by a column of ones. With $p$ nonintercept columns, $q=p+1$ coefficients are collected in $\beta\in\mathbb R^q$. The model is $$y=X\beta+\varepsilon, \tag{8.1}$$ where $\varepsilon\in\mathbb R^n$ is the unobserved error vector. Each column must have a defined measurement scale or encoding. A categorical predictor can contribute several indicator columns, so counting named variables is not always counting coefficients.

When $E(\varepsilon\mid X)=0$, the model specifies the conditional response mean $X\beta$. In an additive model, a slope compares a one-unit change in its design coordinate while the other coordinates are fixed. Whether such a comparison exists in the observed data is a separate question. For strongly dependent features, holding one fixed and moving another can describe combinations with little support. The coefficient then extrapolates within the model even if it is algebraically identified.

The laboratory constructs two correlated predictors with response coefficients $-2$ and $3$. Its simple first-predictor slope is $-0.2$, whereas the adjusted slope is $-2$. Both calculations use the same response rows. The difference occurs because the simple model incorporates the second predictor's association with the first. It is an illustration of adjustment, not a causal estimate or a reconstruction of the source Toyota dataset.

Source discussion: several factors affecting a price and simple and adjusted Toyota regressions.

8.2 Least squares is an orthogonal projection

Minimize $Q(b)=\|y-Xb\|^2$, where $\|v\|^2=v^{\mathsf T}v$ is the squared Euclidean norm. Differentiation gives the normal equations $$X^{\mathsf T}X\widehat\beta=X^{\mathsf T}y. \tag{8.2}$$ Assume $X$ has full column rank $q\leq n$. Then $X^{\mathsf T}X$ is positive definite: for any nonzero $a$, $a^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}Xa=\|Xa\|^2>0$. Consequently the unique solution is $$\widehat\beta=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}y. \tag{8.3}$$ The inverse is a mathematical expression, not a recommended computational recipe. A QR factorization or a singular-value decomposition solves the least-squares problem without explicitly forming the inverse of a potentially ill-conditioned cross-product matrix.

Proposition 8.1 — projection decomposition, proved here. Define $H=X(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}$ and $M=I_n-H$. The fitted response is $\widehat y=Hy$, the residual is $e=My$, and $X^{\mathsf T}e=0$. Both $H$ and $M$ are symmetric and idempotent, meaning $H^2=H$ and $M^2=M$.

Transpose $H$ and use symmetry of $(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}$ to obtain $H^{\mathsf T}=H$. Multiplication gives $$H^2=X(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}(X^{\mathsf T}X)(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}=H.$$ It follows that $M^{\mathsf T}=M$ and $M^2=I_n-2H+H^2=M$. Also $HX=X$ and $MX=0$, so $X^{\mathsf T}My=0$. Equation (8.3) yields $X\widehat\beta=Hy$, and subtraction yields $e=My$. Finally $HM=0$ makes fitted and residual components orthogonal. ∎

For any other coefficients $b$, write $y-Xb=e+X(\widehat\beta-b)$. Orthogonality makes its squared norm equal to $\|e\|^2+\|X(\widehat\beta-b)\|^2$. Full rank makes the second term strictly positive unless $b=\widehat\beta$. This proves global minimality and uniqueness without appealing to a local derivative test alone.

The column space $\operatorname{col}(X)$ is the set of all response vectors representable by this design. A rank-deficient matrix still has a unique projection onto that space, but several coefficient vectors may represent the same projected vector. Software can select a pseudoinverse solution or omit redundant coefficients. Such a convention cannot create unique scientific identification where the design lacks it.

Source derivation: matrix least squares and residual projection. This edition uses $q$ for the full coefficient count, including any intercept.

8.3 What unbiasedness actually assumes

Keep $X$ fixed and full rank. Put $A=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}$. Substituting (8.1) into (8.3) gives the exact identity $\widehat\beta-\beta=A\varepsilon$. Hence $$E(\widehat\beta\mid X)=\beta+A E(\varepsilon\mid X). \tag{8.4}$$ Conditional mean-zero errors therefore imply conditional unbiasedness. The sample normal equations do not imply this assumption: a least-squares residual is orthogonal to the design by construction, even if omitted features make $E(\varepsilon\mid X)$ nonzero.

Let $\Omega=\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon\mid X)$ be the conditional error covariance matrix. Linear transformation of a random vector gives $$\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta\mid X)=A\Omega A^{\mathsf T}. \tag{8.5}$$ When $\Omega=\sigma^2I_n$, this reduces to $\sigma^2(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}$. Gaussianity has not entered either derivation. Unequal variances or correlated errors change the covariance formula; they need not change the coefficient expectation if the conditional mean remains zero.

The residual is $e=M(X\beta+\varepsilon)=M\varepsilon$. It therefore has conditional mean zero and covariance $$\operatorname{Cov}(e\mid X)=\sigma^2M \tag{8.6}$$ in the equal-variance, uncorrelated-error model. This is an $n\times n$ matrix. The scalar expected residual squared length is different: $$E(e^{\mathsf T}e\mid X)=E[\operatorname{tr}(\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\mathsf T}M)\mid X] =\operatorname{tr}(\sigma^2I_n M)=\sigma^2(n-q). \tag{8.7}$$ The trace of $H$ is $\operatorname{tr}[(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}X]=q$, so $\operatorname{tr}(M)=n-q$. Symmetric idempotent matrices have only zero and unit eigenvalues, identifying these traces as the projection dimensions.

For $n>q$, the residual variance estimator $s^2=e^{\mathsf T}e/(n-q)$ is thus unbiased for $\sigma^2$. Even with independent errors, residuals are generally dependent because (8.6) contains off-diagonal entries. Their individual variances are $\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})$, where $h_{ii}$ is the corresponding diagonal element of $H$. This fact is central to the different residual scalings in influence diagnostics.

Source discussion: coefficient moments and residual squared length. Covariance of a vector and expectation of its squared length are kept separate here.

8.4 Gaussian likelihood and exact inference

Now impose $\varepsilon\mid X\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_n)$, with $\sigma^2>0$. Apart from an additive constant, the log likelihood is $$\ell(\beta,\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\log(\sigma^2)-\frac{(y-X\beta)^{\mathsf T}(y-X\beta)}{2\sigma^2}. \tag{8.8}$$ For any fixed positive variance, maximizing this expression minimizes the same least-squares criterion. Thus the coefficient maximum-likelihood estimator is (8.3). For positive residual sum of squares, differentiating the likelihood in $v=\sigma^2$ after fitting the coefficients gives $-n/(2v)+\mathrm{SSE}/(2v^2)=0$, and therefore $$\widehat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}n =\frac{n-q}{n}s^2. \tag{8.9}$$ The coefficient estimators coincide under this Gaussian model, but the unbiased and likelihood variance estimators differ. If the residual sum of squares is zero, the likelihood increases as the variance approaches zero; the stated positive-variance parameter space has no finite maximizer.

Under the Gaussian model, $\widehat\beta\mid X$ is multivariate normal. Orthogonal projection makes $e$ independent of $\widehat\beta$, and $\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-q}$. For coefficient index $j$, the exact null statistic for $H_0:\beta_j=b_j$ is $$T_j=\frac{\widehat\beta_j-b_j}{s\sqrt{[(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}]_{jj}}}\sim t_{n-q}. \tag{8.10}$$ The degrees of freedom count all estimated coefficients, including the intercept. A multiple-regression prediction does not automatically use the simple-model value $n-2$.

A two-sided p-value is the null-model tail probability $\Pr(|T_{n-q}|\geq|T_j^{\mathrm{obs}}|)$. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true. Failure to reject does not establish an absent association; rejection does not establish a causal effect or a useful predictive improvement. The confidence interval describes uncertainty for the coefficient of this specified model under its assumptions.

For a reduced design nested inside a full design, let their ranks be $q_R<q_F<n$, with $r=q_F-q_R$. Under the Gaussian model and the null that the reduced mean is correct, $$F=\frac{(\mathrm{SSE}_R-\mathrm{SSE}_F)/r}{\mathrm{SSE}_F/(n-q_F)}\sim F_{r,n-q_F}. \tag{8.11}$$ The additional fitted subspace has dimension $r$ and is orthogonal to the full residual space. Their independent normal squared lengths produce the numerator and denominator chi-squares. This identifies both why the statistic is nonnegative for truly nested models and why the calibration fails if the candidate models were chosen adaptively without accounting for selection.

For a new design row $x_0^{\mathsf T}$, the fitted mean is $x_0^{\mathsf T}\widehat\beta$. Its estimated variance is $s^2x_0^{\mathsf T}(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}x_0$. An independent new observation adds $s^2$ to that quantity for a prediction interval. Rebuild the new design with exactly the training encoding: a changed category baseline or missing interaction column changes the meaning of $x_0$.

8.6 Exercises with worked solutions

  1. Conceptual — what is fixed? Why can a mileage coefficient change after adding age, and why is the adjusted coefficient not automatically causal?

    Solution

    The simple coefficient summarizes the response change over the ages associated with observed mileage. The adjusted coefficient describes a model comparison at fixed age. Correlation between age and mileage lets an omitted age contribution enter the simple coefficient. Adjustment only concerns included columns; unmeasured confounding, selection, and lack of supported predictor combinations can still defeat a causal interpretation.

  2. Conceptual — vector covariance or squared length? Are $\operatorname{Cov}(e\mid X)$ and $E(e^{\mathsf T}e\mid X)$ interchangeable?

    Solution

    No. The covariance is the matrix $\sigma^2M$, encoding each residual's variance and every pair's covariance. The expected squared length is the scalar trace $\sigma^2(n-q)$ because the residual mean is zero. A scalar cannot replace a matrix in a covariance propagation calculation.

  3. Derivation — unbiased residual variance. Starting from $e=M\varepsilon$, derive $E(s^2\mid X)=\sigma^2$ without Gaussianity.

    Solution

    Under conditional mean-zero errors with covariance $\sigma^2I_n$, $E(\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\mathsf T}\mid X)=\sigma^2I_n$. Since $M$ is symmetric and idempotent, $e^{\mathsf T}e=\operatorname{tr}(M\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\mathsf T})$. Conditional expectation gives $\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(M)=\sigma^2(n-q)$. Division by $n-q>0$ proves the claim. Gaussianity is needed later for the exact chi-square law, not this expectation.

  4. Derivation — coefficient basis change. If $B$ is an invertible $q\times q$ matrix and $X_*=XB$, show the fitted values are unchanged and derive the transformed coefficient.

    Solution

    The two designs span the same column space, so their unique orthogonal projection of $y$ is identical. Set $\widehat\beta_*=B^{-1}\widehat\beta$; then $X_*\widehat\beta_*=XBB^{-1}\widehat\beta=X\widehat\beta$. Full rank ensures this is the unique transformed least-squares coefficient. Coefficients depend on coordinates, whereas fitted vectors depend on the represented space.

  5. Computation — verify the projections. Run the default laboratory and independently check the projection dimensions and the two variance estimates.

    Solution

    The run has $n=8$, $q=3$, trace $H=3$, and trace $M=5$. The constructed discrepancy lies in the residual space and has squared length $8$. Consequently the unbiased variance estimate is $8/5=1.6$ and its Gaussian likelihood counterpart is $8/8=1$. The program asserts idempotence, $MX=0$, and agreement between two least-squares solvers.

  6. Computation — reproduce and audit new-data intervals. Use the two bundled housing files to run the real-data analysis. Compare mean and observation intervals for the first row, and state what would invalidate a cross-language comparison.

    Solution

    The first fitted mean is $75.417637834$. The mean interval is $[71.293693226,79.541582441]$, and the wider observation interval is $[-9.692148739,160.527424407]$. An authentic comparison must match rows, missing-value handling, column names, factor levels and contrasts, intercept, confidence level, and interval type. Agreement on a different dataset or a different training split would not reproduce the source run. A fresh R fit on the downloaded housing files agrees with Python at an absolute tolerance of $10^{-8}$; this checks the conversion while preserving the separate uncertainty about the source file's exact version.

8.7 A fitted surface still needs diagnostic checks

Multiple regression captures additive conditional associations in a chosen design space. Nonlinear features and interactions can enlarge that space while retaining linear coefficients. Neither full rank nor a small residual sum of squares guarantees an adequate error model, supported extrapolation, or stable coefficients. A single influential row can materially change the fitted surface, and a small residual does not by itself exclude influence. The next chapter separates residual size, predictor geometry, and deletion effects.
