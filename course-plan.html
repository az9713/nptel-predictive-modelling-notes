Course architecture

Autonomous production covers all 39 teaching recordings, grouped by topic. All R examples are to be converted to Python, with specific explanations for obstacles.

1. Data, prediction, and correlation

  • Separate quantitative counts, quantitative continuous measurements, and categorical labels; separate cross-sectional, temporal, and panel observations.
  • For finite-variance nonconstant real random variables, correlation is covariance divided by the product of standard deviations and lies in [-1,1] by Cauchy-Schwarz.

Typed features, correlation, scaling, and a nonlinear zero-correlation counterexample.

2. Principal components: variance, geometry, and reconstruction

  • For centered Z and sample covariance S=Z^T Z/(n-1), a unit vector maximizing v^T S v is a leading eigenvector of S.
  • For an orthogonal rank-k projection onto leading covariance eigenvectors, squared reconstruction error equals (n-1) times the sum of discarded eigenvalues; tied eigenvalues need subspace rather than signed-vector comparisons.

Covariance versus correlation PCA, SVD, explained variance, reconstruction, and Python conversion of source R demonstrations.

3. Factor analysis and the limits of latent explanations

  • In x=mu+Lambda f+u with Cov(f)=I, Cov(u)=Psi and Cov(f,u)=0, Cov(x)=Lambda Lambda^T+Psi.
  • For orthogonal Q, Lambda Q and Q^T f preserve the common-factor covariance; factor orientation is not identified by covariance alone.

Factor extraction, rotation, communalities and factor-score conventions; inventory and convert all R calls.

4. Clustering and hierarchical linkage

  • A partition assigns each observation to exactly one nonempty cluster; its usefulness depends on scaling, dissimilarity and the application.
  • Single, complete and average linkage define between-cluster dissimilarity respectively by the minimum, maximum and mean pairwise dissimilarities.

Hand-trace agglomeration and compare linkage rules on the same scaled observations.

5. Ward clustering, dendrograms, and reproducible implementations

  • For Euclidean observations, merging clusters A and B increases within-cluster squared error by |A||B|/(|A|+|B|) times squared centroid distance.
  • A dendrogram cut defines a partition; display leaf order, tie handling and reported linkage heights depend on conventions and must be audited separately from partition agreement.

Translate R hierarchical-clustering examples with explicit Ward conventions, distances, labels and cut levels.

6. K-means as alternating optimization

  • For fixed nonempty assignments, each arithmetic centroid minimizes its cluster's sum of squared Euclidean distances.
  • Nearest-centroid assignment and centroid recomputation do not increase the k-means objective; this does not guarantee a global minimum.

Implement Lloyd iterations and compare source initialization/restart conventions with Python.

7. Simple linear regression: fitting and uncertainty

  • When sum_i(x_i-xbar)^2>0, the intercept-and-slope least-squares minimizer has slope sum_i(x_i-xbar)(y_i-ybar)/sum_i(x_i-xbar)^2 and intercept ybar minus slope times xbar.
  • Under fixed nonconstant predictors and independent Gaussian errors with common variance, the standardized slope estimator using residual variance with n-2 degrees of freedom has a t distribution for n>2.

Fit a simple model, derive the output, and distinguish mean-response from new-observation intervals.

8. Multiple regression and projection geometry

  • For full-column-rank X, the unique least-squares coefficient solves X^T X beta_hat=X^T y, and the residual is orthogonal to col(X).
  • Under E(epsilon|X)=0 and Cov(epsilon|X)=sigma^2 I, beta_hat is conditionally unbiased with covariance sigma^2 (X^T X)^-1; Gaussianity is additional for exact normal-theory inference.

Reproduce multiple-regression examples with QR/SVD-based fits and explicit design matrices.

9. Outliers, leverage, and influence

  • For a rank-q projection H, 0<=h_ii<=1 and sum_i h_ii=q; a large residual and high leverage measure different features of an observation.
  • If h_ii<1, deleting row i changes its fitted residual into the leave-one-out prediction error e_i/(1-h_ii).

Translate source diagnostics and compare residual, leverage, studentization, Cook distance, and refits.

10. Categorical predictors and interpretable contrasts

  • With an intercept, K group indicators sum to the intercept column and are rank deficient; K-1 treatment indicators identify a baseline parameterization when all groups occur.
  • Changing a full-rank basis for the same design-column space changes coefficients but preserves fitted values and residuals.

Convert R formula/factor coding into explicit Python contrasts and verify fitted-value equivalence.

11. Nonlinear features and interaction effects

  • A model polynomial in an observed predictor can remain linear in its regression coefficients.
  • For conditional mean beta0+beta1 x+beta2 z+beta3 xz, the partial effect with respect to x is beta1+beta3 z; main effects depend on the chosen reference values.

Convert R transformations and interactions; compare centered and uncentered equivalent fits.

12. Autocorrelated errors and dependent observations

  • With full-rank fixed X and conditional mean-zero errors, dependence alone does not bias OLS; covariance is (X^T X)^-1 X^T Omega X (X^T X)^-1.
  • The Durbin-Watson statistic is sum_{i=2}^n(e_i-e_{i-1})^2 divided by sum_i e_i^2; its calibration depends on the regression and ordering assumptions.

Audit source dependence tests and simulate the difference between coefficient behavior and invalid iid standard errors.

13. Normality checks and the assumptions behind inference

  • Normality of errors is not required for OLS's algebraic projection or conditional unbiasedness.
  • Under Gaussian homoscedastic errors, Cov(e|X)=sigma^2(I-H); regression residuals are generally dependent and have unequal marginal variances, so an iid normality-test interpretation needs qualification.

Convert source tests and Q-Q diagnostics, record test variants, and compare heavy tails with influential observations.

14. Multicollinearity, identifiability, and conditioning

  • Exact column dependence destroys unique coefficient identification; near dependence can make coefficients unstable even when fitted values are stable.
  • With an intercept and full rank, variance of slope j under homoscedastic errors is sigma^2/[Sxx_j(1-R_j^2)], where R_j^2 is the auxiliary regression fit of that predictor on the others.

Convert source VIF/collinearity diagnostics and inspect singular values, coefficient perturbations and predictions.

15. Heteroscedasticity and variance-aware regression

  • If Omega is known positive definite, minimizing (y-X beta)^T Omega^-1(y-X beta) gives generalized least squares with covariance (X^T Omega^-1 X)^-1 under conditional mean-zero errors.
  • OLS covariance with nonconstant error variances uses a sandwich form; a sample residual-based estimate requires explicit large-sample conditions and a named finite-sample correction.

Translate heteroscedasticity tests, weighted fits and covariance conventions; compare fitted means and uncertainty.

16. Model selection, evaluation, and an end-to-end case study

  • A fitted prediction procedure includes preprocessing and model selection; held-out performance estimates require evaluating the complete procedure without fitting these steps on the held-out outcomes.
  • AIC and prediction error answer different questions; selecting on the test set invalidates its intended role as an untouched assessment sample.

Convert all source case-study and selection examples; add a clearly labeled leakage-safe Python evaluation.

17. Odds ratios and logistic regression

  • If logit P(Y=1|x)=x^T beta, increasing feature j by one unit with all other design coordinates fixed multiplies the odds by exp(beta_j); it does not generally multiply probability by that amount.
  • For independent Bernoulli responses the logistic log-likelihood has gradient X^T(y-pi) and Hessian -X^T W X, W_ii=pi_i(1-pi_i); separation may prevent a finite unpenalized maximum.

Convert source odds/logistic examples, disable unintended penalties, and compare likelihood, uncertainty, calibration and thresholds.

18. Regression discontinuity and local causal identification

  • For sharp treatment D=1{r>=c}, continuity at c of both conditional potential-outcome means and appropriate support identify the jump in E(Y|r) as the treatment effect at the cutoff.
  • A fitted jump is not causal without the identification assumptions; local polynomial estimation additionally depends on bandwidth, specification and an appropriate uncertainty calculation.

Convert any source cutoff example; implement a labeled synthetic sharp-design demonstration with sensitivity checks, without claiming formal robust-bias-corrected inference.
